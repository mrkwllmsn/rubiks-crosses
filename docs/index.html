A one-algorithm cube pattern

Every Face a Cross

Pick a face to watch. Run the same four moves at it until it comes back solid, and the four faces around it are now crosses. Then pick one of those crosses to watch instead and do it again. Two phases, one algorithm, nothing else to memorise.

01

The method

The method runs in two phases, and each phase has an anchor: the one face you keep pointed at yourself while you work. Within a phase the anchor never moves; it only spins on the spot. Between phases you deliberately change it, and that swap is the whole trick. Everything else is the Right-Hand Algorithm and a quarter-turn of the cube, alternating.

01

Choose an anchor, then trigger it three times

Point any face at yourself; white is as good as any. That is your anchor for this phase. Now the Right-Hand Algorithm, the beginner-method staple often just called the trigger: right face up, top face left, then undo both. Three times through, without stopping to look.

02

Spin the cube a quarter turn

Rotate the whole cube 90° clockwise, still looking at the anchor. The anchor doesn't go anywhere; it spins on the spot, and a different side arrives under your right hand. Back to step 1.

Whole cubez
03

Stop when the anchor comes back solid

Exactly 9 rounds. Nothing recognisable appears before then. The cube stays scrambled for 8 rounds and snaps into place on the 9th. Your anchor and the face behind it are solid; the four faces ringing them are crosses.

Round 9 of 92 solid · 4 crosses
04

Change anchor, and do it again

Four crosses, two solid faces. Turn one of the cross faces round to the front and make that your anchor. You stop looking at white now. Run steps 1–3 against the new anchor, all 9 rounds. The two solid faces gain crosses and the four you had keep theirs, so all six carry one.

02

Choosing your two colours

Phase one is predictable enough to aim. Every face keeps its own colour as the background, and takes as its cross the colour that one spin of the cube would bring onto it. Spinning clockwise, that is the face on its left. Set the two colours up in that relationship before you start and the cross comes out exactly as ordered. If you are making a flag rather than the six-cross pattern, stop at the end of phase one: phase two re-colours everything.

Adjacent only

A face can only borrow from a neighbour, so opposite colours never meet: no white cross on yellow, no red on orange. Every other pairing is available.

Four at once, one of them yours

All four faces around the anchor cross over in the same round, each wearing its own left-hand neighbour. Aim the one you care about and let the other three fall where they will.

03

Why 9 rounds

The algorithm folds back

Run R U R' U' six times and the cube returns to solved. So doing it three times lands you exactly halfway, a move that is its own undo. Three is the only count that gives you that.

The cube turn stops it undoing

On its own the half-algorithm would cancel out the moment you repeated it. The z between rounds hands the work to a different pair of faces each time, so it accumulates instead, while the anchor, sitting on the axis you spin around, is never taken apart.

The count is forced

The z repeats every 4 rounds, the algorithm's own pattern on a longer cycle; the two only line up again after 9. Stop early and you have nothing: there is no shortcut through the middle.

05

How this was found

No theory behind it. I was up late pushing sequences around a cube, trying to speed up my RHA. Run something, turn the cube, run it again, see what falls out. Most of what I tried went nowhere; you get a mess, you reset, you try the next thing.

Then I tried the Right-Hand Algorithm three times with a quarter-turn between, kept at it, and the face I was watching came back solid with crosses sitting on every side of it. That was the surprise. The obvious next question was whether the two faces still missing a cross could be dealt with the same way, so I turned one of the finished crosses to the front and ran exactly the same thing at it. They could.

The reason this is easy to miss is that it looks like a dead end for eight rounds. Nothing partial shows up on the way: no half-cross, no hint you are close. The pattern only appears on the ninth. Anyone who stops early, which is the sensible thing to do, never sees it.
